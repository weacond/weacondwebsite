# Valuation From Price to Intrinsic Value

# Chapter 01 · From Price to Value

## Hook

You can buy a share for $100 tomorrow or for $40 next week. The $100 and the $40 are prices. Something else is fixed by the company, not by the market: what the business is actually worth over its remaining life. That something else is *intrinsic value* — the total cash the business will throw to its owners, brought back to today. Every valuation in this book is the act of measuring the gap between those two numbers, and then deciding what to do about the gap.

The single most useful habit a private investor can build is separating these two words in their mind. *Price* is what the market quotes right now; it is a number other people are willing to pay, and it moves every second on every mood. *Value* is what the business is worth; it changes slowly, as the business earns, spends, and grows. Most of the pain in investing comes from confusing them — from treating a price as if it were a value, or a value as if it were a price.

This chapter does one thing: it installs the distinction between price and value as the foundation on which every later chapter stands, and it shows you that "value" is not a feeling but a calculation with inputs you can check.

## Concept

**Simply put:** price is what you pay; value is what you get. The job of valuation is to estimate value, compare it to price, and only then decide whether the deal is good.

**price vs value:**

- *Plain meaning:* Price is the market's current quote for a share — a bid and an offer that other participants set by their mood, information, and cash at hand. Value is the present value of the cash the business will generate and return to its owners over its life, an estimate that belongs to the business and changes slowly.
- *Why it matters:* Price and value move on different clocks. Price jumps on news, rumors, and liquidity; value moves on earnings, margins, and reinvestment. When the two disagree, one of two things is true: the market is wrong, or you are wrong. Valuation is the discipline of finding out which — and never assuming the market's number is the truth.
- *How to use it:* Before you act on any price, ask what the business is worth independent of today's quote. If you cannot answer that question, you do not own an investment — you own a bet on what other people will pay later.

**intrinsic value:**

- *Plain meaning:* The total cash a business will produce for its owners over its remaining life, discounted back to today's dollars at a rate that reflects the risk of that cash. It is an estimate, not an exact number, which is exactly why the discipline treats it as a range and not a point.
- *Why it matters:* Intrinsic value is the only anchor that does not depend on the crowd. Every strategy in this curriculum — value, quality, even much of growth — is a method for estimating intrinsic value faster and more honestly than the market, then buying when price sits below it.
- *How to use it:* Treat your estimate of intrinsic value as a range with a middle and a margin, not a single digit. A single digit invites false precision; a range keeps you honest about how much you actually know.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** the gap between price and value is closed by four forces, and understanding them is the whole of valuation.

1. **Time value of money.** A dollar earned five years from now is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar can itself be invested. Every future cash flow must be pulled back to the present at a discount rate, and the longer the wait, the heavier the pull. This is why two businesses with identical future cash flows can have very different values if the timing differs.

2. **Risk.** Uncertain cash flows are worth less than certain ones, dollar for dollar. The discount rate is the price of that uncertainty — the return you would require to accept the risk of waiting for that cash. Higher risk, higher required return, lower present value.

3. **Compounding and duration.** A business that survives a long time and compounds its earnings has an enormous value, because the later years contribute most of the total. A short-lived or dead business has almost no value no matter how profitable its early years looked. Longevity is worth more than most people credit.

4. **Market error.** Price deviates from value constantly, driven by emotion, information gaps, and incentives. Valuation exploits the gap when price is too low (buy) or too high (sell), and refuses to trade when the two are about equal (hold or stay out). The gap is the edge; the discipline is knowing when the gap is real.

## Historical cases

Three episodes show the price-value gap opening and closing at different speeds, and why the distinction matters for each.

**1. 1929 and the decade after — a value that took a long time to reappear.** At the top, the market priced in permanent prosperity. When the cash flows collapsed, price fell roughly 89 percent from its peak (the Dow from about 381 to about 41). *(source: widely reported; index peak-to-trough figures.)* The lesson is that price can run far ahead of value and then snap back, and that the businesses whose value truly held were the ones whose underlying cash generation survived. The gap was not closed by optimism; it was closed, painfully, by years of lower cash flows.

**2. The 2000 technology correction — a price that outran any value.** At the peak, many internet names traded at price-to-earnings multiples in the hundreds while their cash flows were near zero or negative. *(source: widely reported; era figures.)* Here the gap was not a matter of timing — it was a matter of category. Price was quoting a value that the businesses had not yet earned and, for many, would never earn. The lesson is that a high multiple can encode an assumption of growth so steep that no realistic cash flow can support it, and the collapse closes a gap that math, not mood, had already flagged.

**3. A deep-value recovery — value that the crowd dismissed.** In the 1970s, a style of investing that bought businesses trading far below the present value of their cash flows fell out of fashion as growth and momentum dominated. *(source: widely reported; era performance is broadly described in value-investing literature.)* When the discount rate rose and the growth trade reversed, those deeply discounted businesses outperformed sharply. The lesson is that a wide, persistent gap between price and value can reward the patient and punish the fashionable, often for years.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** The index moves are widely reported peak-to-trough figures, not exact quotes. The 1970s value-versus-growth reversal is a qualitative, broadly documented pattern, not a precise number. The point in all three is the same — the gap between price and value is real, it can be very large, and it closes on the clock of cash flows, not on the clock of mood.

## Numeric example

A company will return $100 of cash to its owners every year for a very long time. How much is that worth today? It depends entirely on the discount rate — the return the investor requires.

At a 5% discount rate, a stream of $100 a year forever is worth $100 / 0.05 = $2,000. At a 10% discount rate, the identical stream is worth $100 / 0.10 = $1,000. The cash flow did not change; the price that makes sense fell by half, purely because the required return doubled.

Now suppose the stream is not forever but $100 a year for ten years. At 5%, the value is about $772; at 10%, about $614. The same principle, a finite version: pull each future dollar back, add them, and the required return is the dial that turns a big number into a smaller one.

The arithmetic point — $100 / rate is the value of a perpetuity — is exact. The ten-year figures use standard present-value factors and are exact to the dollar shown. What this illustrates is the single most important fact in valuation: the same business can be worth twice as much or half as much depending on the rate you require, which is why the discount rate, not the cash flow alone, so often decides the verdict.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking price for value.** Reading the market quote as the answer instead of as one data point. This is the root error; most others follow from it.
- **Demanding a single digit.** Insisting that intrinsic value be one exact number, which creates false precision and false confidence. Value is a range.
- **Ignoring the discount rate.** Comparing two businesses on cash flow alone, as if a risky dollar and a safe dollar were the same. They are not; the rate is what prices the difference.
- **Confusing a high price with a good value.** A stock can be expensive and a great company at once. The verdict comes from comparing price to value, not from either in isolation.

## Different contexts

The price-value distinction shows up differently depending on what you are buying. For a *long-lived, cash-generating business*, value is dominated by the long tail of future cash flows, and the discount rate matters enormously. For a *holding that is near the end of its life* — a mature asset, a declining business — value is dominated by the cash it can throw out and the eventual proceeds, and the long tail shrinks. For *assets that generate little or no cash* — an early-stage venture, a pre-revenue business, a commodity in storage — intrinsic value as a discounted cash flow is hard to pin down, and relative or asset-based methods (later chapters) often matter more. Knowing which context you are in tells you which valuation method is even appropriate, which is itself half the judgment.

## Practical framework and summary

**The disciplined move is:** (1) separate price from value in your mind before you do anything; (2) estimate value as a range by pulling future cash back to today at a risk-based rate; (3) compare the range to the quoted price; (4) act only when the gap is large enough to matter and you understand why it exists; (5) refuse to trade when price and value are close, because there is no edge there.

**Decision rule:** If you cannot state a range for intrinsic value and the reason price differs from it, you are not investing — you are trading. Treat "I do not know the value" as a valid, frequent, and respectable answer.

**If–then:** If the gap is wide and you understand its cause, size the position to the size of your knowledge; if the gap is wide but you cannot explain it, treat the gap as a warning that you are missing something; if the gap is narrow, do nothing.

**Summary in three sentences:** Price is what the market quotes; value is what the business is worth, and the two move on different clocks. Every valuation is an estimate of value, pulled to today by a discount rate and expressed as a range, not a single number. The edge is the gap between them, and the discipline is knowing when the gap is real and large enough to act on.

**One thing you can do today:** Pick one company you own or are watching, write down the price you could buy it at, and write a one-paragraph case for what the business is worth independent of that price — then note which of the four forces (time value, risk, compounding, market error) best explains any gap.
