# Research and Information Literacy

# Chapter 01 · From Data to Decision

## Hook

A reader can know every fact in the world and still make a poor decision, and a reader can know little and make a good one. The difference is not more information. It is how that information is handled: where it came from, how strong the evidence behind it is, and how sure the reader is they are right. This book is about that gap — the distance between having data and knowing what to do with it. It is the meta-layer of the curriculum: every other book produces claims, numbers, and conclusions, and this book teaches how to judge them.

The market is flooded with information. The problem is never a shortage. It is that the information is uneven in quality, mixed with marketing, and easy to misread. A headline, a forecast, a chart pattern, a social post, and an AI answer all arrive in the same confident tone, but they are not the same kind of claim. Treating them as equal is the root error. The reader who learns to separate "a fact" from "a guess" from "an ad" from "a lie" has an advantage that no one can sell — it is a way of reading.

This chapter sets the task and the principle. The four questions that organize the book, the no-prediction principle sharpened to judgment, and the promise that what follows is a way of thinking, not a list of facts.

This chapter does one thing: define the task of information literacy and the four questions it answers — *what do I know, where did I get it, how strong is the evidence, and where could I be wrong* — under the principle that the aim is better judgment and self-protection, not foresight.

## Concept

**Simply put:** Information literacy is the discipline of asking four questions about every claim before acting on it. The reader treats each claim as a claim, not as an instruction, and grades it by its source and its strength.

**The four questions:**

- *Plain meaning:* (1) **What do I actually know?** Separate what is established from what is assumed from what is hoped. (2) **Where did I get it, and how far up the evidence ladder does it sit?** A regulator filing and a forum post are not equal. (3) **How strong is the evidence?** A single data point, a single year, a single survivor is weak; repeated, sourced, and dated evidence is stronger. (4) **Where could I be wrong?** What would make this claim false, and have I looked for that?

- *Why it matters:* Most bad decisions are not from wrong facts but from ungraded facts — a weak source trusted as strong, a single example trusted as a pattern, a marketing claim trusted as an analysis. The four questions are the filter that catches these before they reach the decision.

- *How to use it:* Before any decision, write down the claim, its source, the evidence supporting it, and the evidence against it. If the last box is empty, the claim is untested, not true. The four questions convert a reflexive "I know this" into a graded "I know this to this degree from this source."

**The point.** The task is not to know more. It is to know better — to grade every claim by where it came from and how strong its evidence is, and to hold an answer open to the evidence that could refute it.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** Information literacy works through three forces acting on every claim.

1. **Layering.** Not all claims are the same kind. A fact, a datum, an analysis, an inference, a speculation, and a marketing line sit at different levels of certainty, and they must be read at their own level. Mixing them up is the first error.
2. **Grading.** Sources sit on a ladder — authoritative primary sources at the top, discovery-only sources at the bottom. A claim's strength is bounded by its weakest link in the source chain, not by how confidently it is stated.
3. **Self-correction.** A claim the reader cannot imagine being wrong about is a claim the reader has not tested. Holding a "where could I be wrong" box open is what separates a judgment from a belief.

These three forces do not predict anything. They make the reader a more reliable judge of the claims produced by every other book in this curriculum. That is the whole of the method.

## Historical cases

Three cases show the cost of ungraded information. Each is an illustration; figures are illustrative estimates.

1. **Survivorship in fund selection †.** Suppose 100 funds start a decade, and 60 liquidate or are closed, leaving 40 that survive. A reader who ranks only the 40 survivors sees an average performance that is flattered by the fact that the 60 that failed are invisible. The lesson: the set you observe is not the set that existed; what is missing shapes the average as much as what is present.
2. **A tiny base read as a big number †.** A company reports revenue up 50% year over year. Read as a 50% jump, it sounds strong. Read in full, it may be a move from a very small base to a still-small one. The unit and the base matter as much as the percentage; the same 50% means different things at different scales.
3. **A pattern mistook for a signal †.** A reader sees a stock win five times in a row and concludes the pattern holds. Five coin flips matching in a row happens about 3.1% of the time — rare but common enough that a short streak proves nothing about the next one. A pattern needs a reason and a large sample, not a short run.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** These are illustrative figures used to expose how information can mislead, not forecasts of any asset. They are recomputed below and are not drawn from any source. The reader should check every real-world figure against an authoritative source, never against a headline or a post. († = an illustrative estimate; figures vary by source)

## Numeric example

**Base rate and the streak.** How surprising is a run of identical coin flips?

- Five in a row: 0.5^5 = 3.1%. Uncommon, but it happens often enough that a five-flip run proves nothing about the sixth.
- Ten in a row: 0.5^10 = 0.098%. That is rare enough to make a reader suspect the coin is not fair — but a single ten-flip run is still just as likely to be chance as to be signal.

A claimed win rate of 60% against a 50% reference (60 out of 100 versus 50 out of 100) looks better than base, but it may be noise: the gap is small enough that a small sample could produce it by chance. The lesson is the base rate: any "edge" must be measured against what would happen by chance, and judged by the size of the sample, not by how it feels.

## Common mistakes

- **Confident tone mistaken for certainty.** A claim stated loudly is not a claim proven. Confidence is the source's, not the reader's.
- **One source mistaken for many.** Repeating a claim in ten places is one claim with ten copies; the strength is that of the origin, not the count.
- **Recent mistaken for relevant.** The newest number is not the most important. A single recent quarter or year can dominate a reader's mind while ignoring the longer record.
- **Absence mistaken for evidence.** No news, no complaint, and no warning are not the same as good news, a clean record, or a guarantee. Silence is information, but the wrong kind of reading treats it as a positive.
- **The AI answer mistaken for a source.** An AI output is a synthesis of prior information, not a primary source; it can state a wrong number confidently. The reader, not the AI, is the final verifier.

## Different contexts

- **Retail vs professional.** Professionals have access to L1/L2 sources (filings, audited reports, professional research); a retail reader often starts at L3/L4. The discipline is the same, but the retail reader must climb the ladder further to reach a primary source.
- **Equities vs fixed income vs commodities.** Each asset's core data sits in different primary sources — financial statements for equities, prospectus and credit data for bonds, supply and inventory data for commodities — but all are judged by the same hierarchy and the same four questions.
- **Human vs machine information.** Human sources can be biased, lazy, or conflicted; machine-generated information can hallucinate and cite sources it did not read. Both need the same verification workflow; the human must check the machine and the machine can help check the human.
- **Informed vs uninformed markets.** In a thin, illiquid market, information moves price harder, so the cost of a misread is larger; in a deep market, the same misread is absorbed. Either way, the discipline of grading the claim does not change.

## Practical framework and summary

**The moves:**

1. **Name the claim.** Write the single claim in one sentence. If it cannot be written, it cannot be tested.
2. **Climb to the source.** Trace the claim up the evidence ladder to the highest-quality source you can reach; grade it by that source, not by the headline.
3. **Check the claim.** Provenance, definition, date, unit, sample, base rate — run the checklist before believing.
4. **Open the "where I'm wrong" box.** State the evidence that would make the claim false, and look for it.
5. **Protect the capital.** Route the claim past the protection checks — the intermediary, the counterparty, the scheme, the conflict.

**Decision rule:** A claim is decision-grade only if it survives the source grade, the claim check, and the "where I am wrong" test. A claim that fails any one is a lead, not a fact, and does not drive a decision.

**If–then:** If a claim arrives confidently but I cannot name its source or the evidence against it, then it is untested, and I hold, do not act.

**Summary in three sentences:**
**Information literacy is the discipline of grading every claim by its source and its evidence, and holding it open to refutation.**
**The four questions — what, where, how strong, where I could be wrong — turn a reflexive "I know" into a graded judgment.**
**The aim is better judgment and self-protection, not foresight: the goal is to be a reliable reader of information, not a predictor of outcomes.**

**One thing you can do today:** For the last decision you made from information, write the four questions for it — what you knew, where it came from, how strong the evidence was, and where you could have been wrong — and see whether it would survive the grade.
