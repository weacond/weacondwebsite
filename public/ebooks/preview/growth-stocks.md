# Growth Stocks and Industry Cycles

# Chapter 01 · What Is a Growth Stock? High Growth Is Not Enough

## Hook

In 2000 a company that sold e-mail to businesses was trading at 70 times its earnings — the dot-com bubble — while an oil company at 11 times was "too boring to own." Two years later the e-mail company was nearly worthless and the oil company was rich. Growth, it turns out, is not the same thing as a good investment.

The word "growth" is the most abused word in investing. It means different things to different people, and each meaning points to a different kind of risk. A beginner who learns "buy growth" and nothing else will usually lose money — because growth is not a property of the stock, it is a property of the business, and the stock price already prices in the growth everyone expects.

This chapter does one thing: define what a growth stock actually is, separate the three different meanings of "growth," and show that the number investors should chase is not growth itself but sustainable growth — the rate a business can keep up without borrowing or over-investing. The value-versus-growth philosophy was already settled; here we learn the mechanics of growth.

## Concept

**Simply put:** a growth stock is a company whose earnings or revenue are growing faster than the average, and whose market price already expects that growth to continue. The price is high because the growth is real. The moment the growth slows, the price falls — often far more than the growth did.

**Growth (the three meanings):**
- *Plain meaning:* (1) earnings growth — net income rising year to year; (2) revenue growth — sales rising; (3) economic growth — the business expanding into new markets or products.
- *Why it matters:* A company can have revenue growth (more customers) yet falling earnings (each customer is less profitable). A company can have earnings growth from a one-time gain (a sale of an asset). Only sustainable, recurring earnings growth is worth paying up for.
- *How to use it:* Always ask which of the three you are looking at, and whether it is recurring or a one-time spike. One-time gains inflate a year's earnings; they do not belong in a growth forecast.

**Sustainable growth rate:**
- *Plain meaning:* the highest rate at which a company can grow its earnings forever without raising new money from the market or taking on more debt. The textbook formula is g = ROE × retention ratio — the return on equity multiplied by the fraction of earnings the company keeps rather than pays out.
- *Why it matters:* It is the ceiling on honest growth. A company that grows faster than this number must fund the gap — by issuing new shares (diluting you) or borrowing (adding risk). Growth beyond the sustainable rate is not free; it is financed, and the financing has a cost.
- *How to use it:* Compute g = ROE × retention. If a company's recent growth is far above this number, ask: where is the extra money coming from? If the answer is "we sold shares" or "we took on debt," the growth is borrowing against your ownership.

**The point.** "Growth" has three meanings, only one of them is durable, and the durable one has a ceiling. The beginner's error is confusing fast growth (impressive, expensive, and short-lived) with sustainable growth (slower, cheaper, and the kind that actually compounds over decades).

## Principle

**The mechanism:** three forces decide whether a company's growth is worth paying for.

1. **Growth is already in the price.** The price of a growth stock is a bet that the growth continues. You are not buying the growth that already happened; you are buying the growth that has not yet happened. The better the recent growth, the more the market has already paid for it.
2. **The faster and more certain the expected growth, the higher the price.** A 30%-growth company costs many times its earnings; a 5%-growth company costs few. The price and the expected growth are locked together — so the question is never "is it growing?" but "is the growth justifying the price?"
3. **Growth that outruns the sustainable rate is financed by dilution or debt.** Beyond g = ROE × retention, every extra percent of growth is borrowed from the owners — either by selling new shares (which shrinks your slice) or by taking on debt (which adds the risk Book 18 studies).
4. **The growth's duration matters more than its speed.** A company that grows 15% for 20 years beats one that grows 40% for 3 years and collapses. The beginner chases the 40%; the durable winner is the slow, long runway.

## Historical cases

1. **The dot-com peak, 2000.** At the top, the Nasdaq 100's price-to-earnings ran above 100 times — the market was paying for growth that would never materialize. By October 2002 the index had fallen about 78%. The growth was real in the moment; it was not real enough to justify the price. Source: market data, Nasdaq 100, Mar 2000 to Oct 2002.
2. **Oil and gas through the 2000s.** At the 2000 top oil traded near 11 times earnings — "too cheap to be interesting" by the standards of the day. Over the next years, as commodity prices rose, those same cheap stocks paid handsomely. Slow, certain growth, priced low, outperformed fast, uncertain growth, priced high. Source: sector history, illustrative.
3. **A high-retention technology compounder (illustrative).** A company earning a 30% return on equity and reinvesting 50% of its earnings sustains g = 30% × 0.50 = 15% — for decades, without dilution. That is the growth that compounds: 15% a year for 30 years turns 1 into about 66. Source: arithmetic, 1.15^30 ≈ 66×.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** The Nasdaq 100 2000-to-2002 decline is a documented market fact; the oil and high-retention compounder cases are illustrative reconstructions used to show the principle, not a claim about any one named company.

## Numeric example

A beginner looks at two companies. Company A grew earnings 40% last year; Company B grew 12%. The beginner buys A — "40% is clearly better." Compute the sustainable rate.

- Company A: ROE 40%, but it reinvests only 25% of earnings (pays out the rest as dividend), and the extra 40% growth came mostly from borrowing and issuing new shares. Sustainable g = 40% × 0.25 = 10%. The 40% was financed; it will not last.
- Company B: ROE 30%, reinvests 40%. Sustainable g = 30% × 0.40 = 12%, with no new debt and no dilution.

Company B's slower growth is the more durable growth, and it can be paid for at a higher price without the same risk. The number to chase is not last year's 40% — it is the 12% that will keep compounding for decades.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying "growth" without separating the three meanings** — paying up for revenue growth while earnings are flat, or for a one-time earnings spike that will not recur.
- **Ignoring that growth is already priced** — buying a stock because it grew 40%, not realizing the 40% is the reason the price is high; the next 40% is what you are really paying for.
- **Chasing speed over duration** — preferring a company that grows 40% for a few years over one that grows 12% for decades.
- **Missing the financing** — not asking where the growth is funded from; growth above the sustainable rate is dilution or debt, which costs the owner.
- **Confusing a growth stock with a value decision** — a fast-growing company can still be expensive; growth is the business property, valuation is the separate question (Book 10).

## Different contexts

- **Early-stage vs mature growth.** A young company can grow 50% a year because the base is small; a mature company grows 8% off a huge base. Both are "growth," but the durability and the price behave very differently.
- **Emerging vs developed markets.** Emerging-market growth is often faster but more volatile and more exposed to currency and policy; developed-market growth is slower but steadier. The sustainable-growth test applies to both.
- **Cyclical vs non-cyclical growth.** For a cyclical business, "growth" is meaningless in a single year — the earnings swing with the cycle (the next chapters). A cyclical company can look like a growth stock in a boom and a collapse in a bust.

## Practical framework and summary

**The moves**
1. Separate the three meanings of growth — revenue, earnings, economic — and check that the growth you see is recurring, not a one-time spike.
2. Compute the sustainable rate: g = ROE × retention. If recent growth is far above it, ask where the extra money is coming from.
3. Ask whether the growth is already priced. You are buying the growth that has not happened, not the growth that did.
4. Prefer duration over speed — a slow, long runway beats a fast, short one.
5. Keep growth separate from valuation — a fast grower can still be expensive.

**Decision rule:** Do not buy "growth." Buy sustainable, recurring, un-priced, and durable growth — and check that the growth is funded without diluting you or loading you with debt.

**If–then:**
- If a company's recent growth is far above g = ROE × retention → the growth is financed; treat it as dilution or debt, not as permanent.
- If you are buying a stock because it just grew → you are paying for the past; you are really paying for the (uncertain) future.
- If two companies grow at similar rates → the one with the longer durable runway and the cheaper price is the better growth investment.

**Summary in three sentences:**
A growth stock is a company whose earnings are growing faster than average and whose price already expects that growth to continue — so the question is never "is it growing?" but "does the growth justify the price?" The beginner's three errors are confusing the three meanings of growth, ignoring that growth is already priced, and chasing speed over duration. The single most useful number is the sustainable growth rate, g = ROE × retention — the ceiling on honest growth, and the one number to check before you pay up.

**One thing you can do today:** Pick one company you admire for "growing," and compute g = ROE × retention. Compare it to the growth the market is pricing in. If the priced-in growth is far above the sustainable rate, you are being asked to pay for a future the business may not deliver.
