# Commodities Gold and Real Assets

# Chapter 01 · Two Ways to Own a Commodity

## Hook

Imagine two people betting on the same thing: the price of crude oil over the next year. One buys a tanker and fills it with barrels; the other opens a position in a commodity fund. Same bet on the price, two completely different machines.

One owns a *physical thing* with weight, storage, and spoilage. The other owns a *paper claim* that tracks the price without any barrels at all. Confuse them, and you will misprice every commodity decision that follows.

This chapter does one thing: separate the two ways to own a commodity so every later chapter has a clean foundation.

## Concept

A commodity is a raw material that is fungible — a barrel of crude, an ounce of gold, a bushel of wheat — and that trades on price, not on the individual unit.

**Simply put:** owning a commodity is different from owning a business that produces it.

**physical vs financial exposure:**
- *Plain meaning:* physical exposure means you actually hold the thing; financial exposure means you hold a contract or fund that tracks its price.
- *Why it matters:* physical carries storage, insurance, and spoilage; financial carries leverage and rollover costs.
- *How to use it:* pick the one that matches your goal — a price view is best held financially.

**The point.** Before you ask "which commodity," ask "which exposure," because the two behave very differently.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** the two exposures differ along three forces.

1. **Income.** A barrel or an ounce pays nothing. A producer stock pays a dividend and a bond pays a coupon. Commodities are the one major asset class with no internal cash flow.
2. **Carrying cost.** Physical ownership costs money to hold: storage, insurance, transport. Financial exposure turns that into a *roll cost* (Chapter 10), not a storage bill.
3. **Leverage.** Physical you buy with cash. Financial exposure is usually bought on margin, which amplifies both gain and loss.

## Historical cases

- **1973 oil shock, oil ~+300% (illustrative).** A physical shortage drove the price up; anyone who held paper oil claims made far more than the holder of a full tanker, who also paid to store it. The lesson: the price move and the carrying cost are separate.
- **2008 commodity boom, industrial metals +100% or more (illustrative).** The demand shock lifted metals; the financial exposure amplified the move through leverage, while the physical owner also fought logistics and storage. The lesson: leverage and logistics ride the same wave in opposite directions.
- **2020 silver and gold, metals −30% to −50% over months (illustrative).** The crash hit every exposure, but the margin-financed positions were forced to sell first, accelerating the drop. The lesson: leverage decides who gets out.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** these are illustrative magnitudes to teach the mechanism, not a trading record.

## Numeric example

Compare the two ways to express the same bet on one barrel.

- **Physical:** you pay $70 and add, say, $3 of storage and insurance to a year later. To keep the barrel costs you $3 more; to give it up you must find a buyer.
- **Financial:** you post $7,000 of margin on a $70,000 contract (10x) and face no storage bill, but a 10% move in price is a 100% move in your margin.

**Decision rule:** use physical only when you need the thing itself; use financial exposure when you need the price.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing the producer with the thing.** Owning an oil company is owning a business, not owning oil (Chapter 6). The two diverge when margins move.
- **Forgetting the carry.** A financial "free" position still pays a roll cost (Chapter 10). Free-looking is not free.
- **Ignoring leverage.** The margin that makes the gain big also makes the loss fatal (Chapter 9).
- **Treating commodities like bonds.** They pay no income. Expecting a "yield" from a barrel is a category error.

## Different contexts

- **An inflation hedge** is often held physically (gold, a small commodity basket) because the point is survival, not efficiency.
- **A trade or hedge** is held financially because the point is the price move, not the thing.
- **A producer stock** belongs to the equity world, not the commodity world, even though its price reacts to commodities.

## Practical framework and summary

The moves:
1. Name the bet: "I am betting on the *price* of X," or "I need the *thing* X."
2. If the price, choose financial exposure and learn its roll and margin (Chapters 9–11).
3. If the thing, choose physical and accept its carrying cost.
4. If a producer, treat it as an equity, not a commodity (Chapter 6).

**Decision rule:** the exposure follows the goal; the goal is "the thing" or "the price," never both at once.

**If–then:** if you cannot hold the thing, own the price financially; if the move is a one-time view, do not build storage for it.

**Summary in three sentences:** A commodity can be owned physically, as a real thing with carrying cost, or financially, as a leveraged claim on its price. The two exposures differ on income, carrying cost, and leverage, and they answer different questions. Picking the exposure before the instrument keeps every later decision honest.

**One thing you can do today:** write down whether your next commodity decision is "I need the thing" or "I need the price," and let that sentence choose the instrument.
