# Financial Statements and Accounting

# Chapter 01 · Reading Financial Statements: Why Accounting Discipline Matters

## Hook

Every company in the world, from the corner shop to the largest bank, keeps the
same kind of book. It records what it owns, what it owes, what it earned, and
what it spent. These records are its financial statements, and for decades they
have been the single most important document an investor reads. A stock price
can lie to you in a minute, but a set of statements, read honestly, is the
closest thing to a confession a company will ever make.

Here is the first and most dangerous idea. Numbers on a page are not the same
thing as what is really happening inside the business. Accounting is a language,
and every language can be stretched. A company that follows the rules exactly
and a company that bends the rules can sometimes print statements that look
almost identical. The rules do not force honesty; they only define the
permissible range of expression. Two honest but different choices can describe
the same business in two ways, and one of them may make the business look far
healthier than it is.

There is a second idea, and it is the reason this book exists. Over the last
century, a long trail of famous companies has failed not because they could not
sell anything, but because what their numbers claimed to be was not what was
really true. When that gap was exposed, shareholders did not lose a little.
They lost almost everything, fast.

This chapter does one thing: it establishes why a single skill — reading a
company's statements and trusting them only as far as they are earned — is the
foundation of everything that follows, and it gives you the questions you will
ask for the rest of the book.

## Concept

**Simply put: the three financial statements — the balance sheet, the income
statement, and the cash flow statement — are three views of the same business,
taken at different angles and times; your job is to read all three together, in
tension with one another, because no one statement alone can be trusted.**

**Financial statements:**
- *Plain meaning:* A standardized set of reports a company publishes, showing what
  it owns and owes (balance sheet), what it earned and spent over a period
  (income statement), and how cash actually moved in and out (cash flow
  statement).
- *Why it matters:* These are the raw material of every valuation and every
  investment decision. Every return you estimate is built on numbers these
  statements report.
- *How to use it:* Never read one statement alone. A company can look great on
  one and be in trouble on another. The truth lives in the tension between them.

**Accounting standards (GAAP and IFRS):**
- *Plain meaning:* The rulebooks that govern how numbers are recorded. US GAAP is
  the rulebook used in the United States; IFRS is used in most other
  jurisdictions. Both require accrual accounting and differ in details.
- *Why it matters:* Without a shared rulebook, one company's "profit" and
  another's would mean different things, and comparison would be impossible.
- *How to use it:* Before comparing two companies, check which rulebook each
  follows. Differences in the rules can create differences in the numbers that
  have nothing to do with the business.

**Accrual accounting:**
- *Plain meaning:* Recording economic events when they happen, not when cash
  changes hands. A sale is counted when the good ships, even if payment arrives
  later.
- *Why it matters:* Accruals are what make the income statement a measure of
  performance rather than a measure of cash in the till. But the same feature is
  also where most manipulation hides.
- *How to use it:* Always read the income statement alongside the cash flow
  statement. Accruals explain the gap between the two, and a widening gap is the
  first red flag a disciplined reader looks for.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** the three statements are not three separate facts. They are
three projections of one three-dimensional object, the business, onto different
axes.

1. *The balance sheet is a snapshot.* It is the business frozen at a single
   moment — assets on one side, liabilities and equity on the other, forced to
   balance by definition. It answers "what is the company worth right now, and
   who has a claim on it?"
2. *The income statement is a film.* It is the business moving across a period —
   revenue minus expenses, ending in net income. It answers "did the business
   create value during this time?"
3. *The cash flow statement is the truth-teller.* It strips out every accrual
   and shows only what actually entered and left the cash account. It answers
   "is there really cash behind the story, or is it only on paper?"

The power, and the danger, is in the relationships between the three. Net income
on the income statement and the cash from operations on the cash flow statement
should move roughly together over time. When a company reports rising profit but
falling operating cash, something has moved into an accrual — a receivable, an
inventory, a capitalized expense — and that is exactly where trouble hides.

A second mechanism is the bridge between the statements. The net income at the
bottom of the income statement is the top line of the operating section of the
cash flow statement, and the ending cash on that statement becomes the cash on
the next balance sheet. Follow that chain and you can watch the whole business
move, quarter by quarter, the way you would watch water flow through a system.
That continuity is what lets a careful reader catch an anomaly: a number that
does not connect to the ones before it is a number that has been forced to look a
certain way.

## Historical cases

The lesson of accounting is not taught by companies that failed to sell. It is
taught by companies that sold plenty, looked fine on paper, and then revealed that
the paper was false.

- *Enron (United States, filed for bankruptcy in December 2001.)* For years the
   company reported strong earnings, yet it was hiding debts and inflating
   revenues through entities that were not on its own books. When the truth
   emerged, a company that had been valued at roughly $74 billion in market
   capitalization collapsed within weeks, and the resulting bankruptcy was one of
   the largest in U.S. history. The conclusion: a balance sheet can be made to
   omit obligations that are, economically, the company's own. *(Source:
   widely reported figures, 2001–2002.)*
- *WorldCom (United States, fraud exposed in 2002.)* The company reported years
   of steady profit, but a large portion of that profit had been created by
   recording operating costs as capital investments, which moved the expense out
   of the income statement and onto the balance sheet. The fraud, one of the
   largest in history, involved amounts on the order of $11 billion. The
   conclusion: an expense can be hidden simply by deciding it is an asset.
   *(Source: widely reported figures, 2002.)*
- *Satyam (India, collapse announced January 2009.)* The company's chairman
   admitted that figures for revenue, cash, and debt had been fabricated, in what
   was described as one of the largest accounting frauds outside the United
   States, with losses on the order of $1.6 billion. The conclusion: in a
   setting where external oversight was weaker, the fabrication could persist for
   years before detection. *(Source: widely reported figures, 2009.)*

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** the dollar magnitudes above are widely reported
> figures from the time, rounded to the order of size. They are used here to
> show the *scale* of what a false statement can cost, not to give a precise
> audit number. Read them as "on the order of" rather than exact.

## Numeric example

Suppose a company reports the following for two consecutive years, in simplified
form. The point is to practice reading the three statements in tension, not to
compute a valuation.

Year A: revenue 100, net income 15, cash from operations 14.
Year B: revenue 110, net income 22, cash from operations 4.

A quick reader sees a company whose profit jumped from 15 to 22, up nearly half.
A disciplined reader stops at Year B. Revenue rose by 10, which is sensible.
Profit rose by 7, which is also plausible. But cash from operations fell from 14
to 4, even as profit rose. Where did the profit go? It did not leave as cash. It
went somewhere on the balance sheet — into receivables that have not yet been
collected, or into inventory that has not yet sold. The profit exists on paper,
but the cash does not yet exist. A reader who trusts only the income statement
congratulates the company. A reader who reads all three raises a question: is
this profit real, or is it waiting to come back?

This is the entire discipline in miniature. The single most valuable habit an
investor can build is the refusal to read any one statement alone, and the
reflex to ask, for every number that looks good, "where is the cash?"

## Common mistakes

- *Trusting a single statement.* Seeing strong profit and stopping. The mistake
  is treating one projection of the business as the whole business.
- *Confusing profit with cash.* The most common and most costly error. A company
  can be profitable and still run out of cash, because profit is an accrual and
  cash is a fact.
- *Ignoring the change, reading only the level.* A single year looks fine. The
  story is in how the number moved relative to the prior years and relative to
  the other statements.
- *Assuming the rules guarantee truth.* The rules define a range of acceptable
  numbers; they do not force the most conservative one. Two compliant companies
  can look very different.
- *Comparing across rulebooks.* Reading a US GAAP company and an IFRS company as
  if the numbers were directly comparable, when the recording rules differ.

## Different contexts

- *A startup with losses.* The income statement shows a negative net income,
  which is normal and not a red flag. The cash flow statement matters more here,
  because a young company can survive years of losses if its cash is not running
  out. The balance sheet's cash and debt lines dominate the read.
- *A mature, steady company.* Profit and operating cash should move closely
  together. A persistent gap is far more concerning here than at a startup,
  because there is no "it is just the growth phase" excuse.
- *A capital-heavy company (utilities, airlines, manufacturing).* The cash flow
  statement's investing section, full of large capital expenditures, dominates.
  Free cash flow, not net income, is the true measure of what owners can take.
- *A bank or insurance company.* The balance sheet is the story. These firms
  hold and manage debt as their core product, so the usual "debt is a warning"
  heuristic does not apply the same way. Different rules of reading, same three
  statements.

## Practical framework and summary

**Numbered moves:**
1. *Always read all three together.* No single statement is trustworthy on its
   own; the truth is in their tension.
2. *Track the bridge.* Follow net income from the income statement into the
   operating cash flow, and ending cash into the next balance sheet. A broken
   bridge is a warning.
3. *Ask "where is the cash?"* for every strong profit figure. If profit rises
   and cash does not, the profit has been parked in an accrual.
4. *Check the rulebook.* Confirm GAAP versus IFRS before comparing any two
   companies.
5. *Read the trend, not the level.* One year is a frame; three or five years is
   the film.
6. *Watch the gap between profit and operating cash.* A widening gap over time is
   the single most useful red flag in all of accounting.

**Decision rule:** If a company's profit and its operating cash flow move
together, the numbers are likely telling the truth; if they diverge for more
than one year, treat the profit with skepticism and look for where the money
actually is.

**If–then:** If you can read the cash flow statement and it agrees with the
income statement, proceed to value the company with confidence. If the two
disagree, your first job is not to value but to investigate, because a false
statement is the one thing that can turn a good-looking investment into a total
loss.

**Summary in three sentences:**
The three financial statements are three views of one business, and none of them
can be trusted alone. Accounting rules define a range of acceptable numbers, not
a single honest one, so two compliant companies can look very different. The one
skill that prevents the most damage is reading all three statements together, in
tension, and asking where the cash is.

**One thing you can do today:** Take the last annual report of any company you
own or admire, find its income statement and its cash flow statement, and compare
the net income to the operating cash flow for the last three years. If they
roughly match, the numbers are trustworthy. If they drift apart, you now know
exactly where to start looking.
