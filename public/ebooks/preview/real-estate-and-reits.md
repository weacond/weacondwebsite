# Real Estate and REITs

# Chapter 01 · The Two Kinds of Real Estate Ownership

## Hook

A house, a warehouse, a skyscraper, and a parcel of farmland share one thing: they are physical things that sit in one place, produce income, and last a long time. Yet when an investor says "real estate," they may mean two very different instruments. One is a building you can walk into; the other is a share in a company that owns buildings, traded on an exchange like a stock. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this field, because they are priced, financed, and taxed differently.

This chapter does one thing: it separates direct real estate from a REIT and explains why every later chapter treats them as two instruments, not one.

## Concept

**Simply put:** there are two ways to own income-producing property. **Direct real estate** is a specific physical asset you own outright or with a loan; you collect its rent, pay its expenses, and bear its risks one by one. A **REIT** — a real estate investment trust — is a publicly traded company that owns a portfolio of such assets and passes most of its income to shareholders as distributions. You do not own the building; you own a slice of the company.

The distinction is the spine of the book. Read it as three claims:

- *Two instruments:* direct property and a REIT are both "real estate" but behave as different assets.
- *Why it matters:* a property is valued on its own cash flow; a REIT is valued like a company, and the two can diverge.
- *How to use it:* before analyzing any real-estate investment, name which of the two you hold.

**The point.** Real estate is not one asset but two lenses on the same income stream — one at the level of a single building, one at the level of a company that owns many. Holding the two lenses apart is the first skill.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** both instruments rest on the same economic engine — property that earns rent, financed partly by debt, valued by what the market will pay for that rent. Three forces decide how each is experienced:

1. **The income source.** Rent from tenants is the cash both instruments ultimately depend on; everything else is built on top of it.
2. **The debt.** Most owners do not pay the full price in cash; a loan multiplies both the return and the risk.
3. **The vehicle.** Owning a building directly exposes you to that building; owning a REIT spreads you across many buildings and adds a management layer in between.

## Historical cases

The split between direct and listed real estate is not new.

- **The 1960 U.S. REIT Act** allowed companies that mostly own and operate income property to pass income to shareholders largely free of corporate tax, on the condition that they distribute most of it. Magnitude: it created the modern REIT as a mass, tradable instrument. Conclusion: the "company that owns buildings, taxed like a pass-through" was a deliberate invention, not a natural one. †
- **Direct-property ownership** has a far older history — individuals and families holding and renting buildings across centuries. Magnitude: wealth in land and buildings pre-dates public markets. Conclusion: direct ownership is the older, more granular form; the REIT is the newer, pooled form. (illustrative historical framing; figures vary by source)
- **The 2007–2009 financial crisis** hit both, but through different channels. Magnitude: falling property values and frozen credit struck direct owners and REITs at once, but REITs could not raise or roll over debt as easily. Conclusion: a shared economic shock does not mean a shared experience — the vehicle matters. †

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** the 1960 REIT Act, the long history of direct ownership, and the 2007–2009 credit squeeze are cited as directional, historical illustrations; verify exact figures and dates for any use. († = an illustrative estimate; figures vary by source)

## Numeric example

Two investors each control property that earns $500,000 a year of rent. They reach it two ways.

- **Direct owner:** buys the building for $10,000,000 with a loan. She collects the rent, pays the expenses, and bears every shock of that one building. Her income and risk are concentrated in a single asset she must manage.
- **REIT shareholder:** buys $10,000,000 of a REIT that owns dozens of such buildings. She receives a distribution from the pooled income and is spread across many properties and locations, with a management team running the assets.

Same income engine, two instruments. The direct owner trades concentration for control; the REIT holder trades control for diversification and liquidity. This trade — and the fact that the two can be priced very differently — is what the rest of the book unpacks.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a REIT as "just a house."** A REIT is a company that owns houses; it is priced and taxed like a company, and can trade at a discount to the value of what it owns.
- **Assuming they move together.** A direct building is valued on its own rent; a REIT is valued like a stock. In a crisis the gap between a REIT's price and the value of its assets can widen sharply.
- **Ignoring the vehicle.** Liquidity, taxes, management fees, and leverage behave differently across the two; a return that looks similar can hide very different costs.
- **Thinking "real estate is always a safe store of value."** It can be a steady income, but it can also fall sharply in value when rents or financing turn against it.

## Different contexts

- **A long-term owner of a single building** is a direct investor: concentrated, illiquid, hands-on, and able to capture full upside and full downside of one asset.
- **A REIT shareholder** is a pooled investor: liquid, diversified, hands-off, and exposed to a management layer and to company-level forces.
- **For a household,** the direct route often means a home or a rental the owner lives in or manages; the REIT route means buying real estate the way one buys a stock, without owning a building.
- **For a large or institutional investor,** REITs and direct property are separate lines on a balance sheet, with different valuations, financing, and tax treatment.

## Practical framework and summary

**The moves:**

1. **Name the instrument** — direct property or a REIT — before naming the price.
2. **Ask what you actually own** — a building, or a share in a company that owns buildings.
3. **Expect the two to differ** in liquidity, leverage, taxes, and how they react to a shock.

**Decision rule:** if you can say whether you hold a property or a company, you can start reading its return, cost, and risk correctly.

**If–then:** if two real-estate investments look similar in yield, check the vehicle first, because the route changes what the yield is worth.

**Summary in three sentences:** Real estate is two instruments — a direct building and a REIT that owns buildings. Both earn rent and use leverage, but they are priced, financed, and taxed differently. Holding the two apart is the foundation for everything that follows.

**One thing you can do today:** the next time you see the words "real estate," ask which kind — a building or a company that owns buildings — because that single question decides how to value it, finance it, and tax it.
