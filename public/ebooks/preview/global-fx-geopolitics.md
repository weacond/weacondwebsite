# Global FX Geopolitics

# Chapter 01 · From One Country to the World

## Hook

Most investing is taught inside a single country. The numbers you have learned so far - a portfolio's risk, a bond's duration, a company's value - assume you are holding an asset whose cash flows and whose price are both denominated in one currency and governed by one legal system. Cross that border and three new forces arrive at once: currency, country, and geopolitics.

Currency means your foreign asset pays you in a money that can move against your own. Country means the legal and economic system that underwrites your asset can change its rules, default, or impose controls. Geopolitics means the relationship between nations can shift in ways that reprice trade, energy, and capital across the whole system at once.

You do not need to master any of the three before you start. This chapter gives you the three forces and the one principle that governs all of them. It does not teach how to predict a currency or time a geopolitical shock - the rest of this book teaches the mechanism of each force; the principle, which this chapter states, is that you explain the transmission, you do not bet on the event.

This chapter does one thing: it sets up the three cross-border forces - currency, country, geopolitics - and the rule that you track the channel between a shock and a price, never the shock itself.

## Concept

**Simply put: when you invest across a border, you are exposed to three new forces at once - currency, country, and geopolitics - and the discipline is the same as every other force in the curriculum: understand the channel, not the event.**

**Cross-border exposure:**

- *Plain meaning:* the exposure that appears when an asset is in a foreign currency or a foreign legal system, on top of the local exposure you already have.
- *Why it matters:* a foreign asset's total return is the local return multiplied by the currency movement, and it is subject to a country's rules and to geopolitical shocks that your home asset does not face.
- *How to use it:* add the currency, country, and geopolitics layers to your analysis; never treat a foreign asset as if it were a domestic one.

**The point.** Cross-border exposure is not a single new risk but three, and they can move you even when the underlying company is fine.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** the three forces act through channels, and the discipline is to follow the channel, not to predict the shock.

1. **Currency force:** a foreign asset's return in your money is (1 + local return) × (1 + currency change) − 1. The currency term can add to or wipe out the local return, and it is driven by interest-rate and inflation differences (Book 19) - so it is not an extra risk but a second, correlated one.
2. **Country force:** the legal and economic system that underwrites the asset can default on its debt, impose capital controls, make a currency non-convertible, or restrict foreign ownership. Each changes the cash flows you expected, and none of it is priced by a domestic model.
3. **Geopolitics force:** relations between nations shift trade, energy, and capital across the whole system - sanctions close a trade channel, war disrupts supply, and a shock in one country transmits to others through trade and capital flows.
4. **The governing principle:** you explain the channel from a shock to a price - a currency move, a default, a sanctions effect - not the event itself. This is the same no-prediction principle from Book 19, applied cross-border: the model tells you what a shock *does* to a price, not *whether* it will happen.
5. **The trap:** the cross-border version is especially tempting to forecast, because a war or a rate difference is a visible, dramatic event. The discipline holds: the model is a channel, the event is unpriceable.

## Historical cases

Three cases show the three forces acting through channels, and why the event was not the thing to predict.

1. **The 1997 Asian currency crisis** †: a cluster of Asian economies with large capital inflows saw those flows reverse, currencies collapsed, and equity markets fell - the channel was capital outflow → currency depreciation → local-currency asset losses, not any single prediction. The lesson was the channel, not a forecast.
2. **The 2001 Argentine default** †: a sovereign that could not service its debt defaulted; bondholders recovered far less than 100%, and the channel was sovereign default → forced loss on the debt, not a market timing call.
3. **The 2022 strong-dollar year** †: a large rise in the dollar's value made dollar-denominated foreign assets cheaper for foreign holders and stressed emerging-market borrowers - the channel was currency appreciation → reduced foreign-asset value and harder debt service, not a price forecast.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** the magnitudes and dates are illustrative or need verification; the mechanism is the point. The channel - capital outflow, sovereign default, currency appreciation - is the thing to model, and the event is the thing you cannot time. († = an illustrative estimate; figures vary by source)

## Numeric example

**Cross-border return compounding - the currency force alone.** You own a foreign asset that returns +10% in its local currency. Two scenarios, same local return, different currency moves.

- **Currency falls 10% against your money:** total = 1.10 × 0.90 − 1 = **−1.0%**. A strong local year becomes a loss because the currency gave it back. †
- **Currency rises 5%:** total = 1.10 × 1.05 − 1 = **+15.5%**. The currency adds to the return. †

The same +10% local return produces −1.0% or +15.5% depending on the currency. That 16.5-point spread is the currency force: it is a second return stream, and it can dominate the first.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the currency:** treating a foreign asset's local return as your return, and forgetting the currency layer.
- **Predicting the event:** trying to forecast a war, a default, or a currency move, and building a position on it - the opposite of the discipline.
- **Double-counting the rate:** assuming a high foreign interest rate is free money, when the expected currency move can offset it (the carry is not a gift).
- **Confusing the country with the company:** assuming a strong company is safe from its country's rules, defaults, or capital controls.
- **Assuming diversification is free:** treating a foreign holding as automatically diversifying, when it can be highly correlated with your home market.

## Different contexts

- **A small investor with one home market:** the currency and country forces may dominate; a foreign holding in a weak, volatile currency can lose value even as the local market rises.
- **A large investor with many markets:** currency and country forces can diversify, but only if the markets are genuinely uncorrelated - correlation rises in a global stress, exactly when you need the diversification most.
- **An investor in a high-interest-rate foreign market:** the carry may attract, but the expected currency depreciation can erase it; the force is the differential, not the rate.
- **An investor exposed to a commodity or energy import:** the geopolitics force bites through the energy and trade channels, not through a direct equity link.

## Practical framework and summary

**The moves:**
1. **Add the three layers:** to any foreign holding, add currency, country, and geopolitics on top of the local analysis.
2. **Model the channel:** for each force, trace the path from the shock to the price - a currency move, a default, a sanctions effect - and stress that path.
3. **Resist the event:** do not build a position on a forecast of a war, a default, or a rate change; the model is a channel, the event is unpriceable.
4. **Check the correlation:** confirm the foreign holding actually diversifies; correlation rises in stress.
5. **Know the country's rules:** understand the default, capital-control, convertibility, and ownership risks before you hold the asset.

**Decision rule:** you are allowed to hold foreign exposure for its mechanism - a different return stream, a diversification, a currency view - but never as a bet on a geopolitical or country *event*; the value is in the channel you understand, not the shock you guessed.

**If–then:**
- If a foreign return looks attractive in local currency, then add the currency layer before you trust it - the total return is local times currency.
- If a high foreign rate tempts you, then check the expected currency move - the carry may be offset.
- If a geopolitical story is in the news, then find the economic channel it transmits through, and do not trade the headline.

**Summary in three sentences:**
Cross-border investing adds three forces - currency, country, and geopolitics - that your domestic analysis does not see, and the discipline is to model the channel between a shock and a price, never the event itself. The currency force alone can turn a strong local year into a loss or a modest one into a large gain, so the total return is always local times currency. The country and geopolitics forces work through defaults, controls, trade, and energy, and you follow each channel rather than forecasting the shock.

**One thing you can do today:** on any foreign holding you own or consider, write the three forces explicitly - the currency layer, the country's rules, and the geopolitical channel - and state, for each, the path by which a shock reaches your price. That is the whole of cross-border discipline, and it is the same as the no-prediction principle from Book 19, applied across a border.
