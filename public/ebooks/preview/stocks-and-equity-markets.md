# Stocks and Equity Markets

# Chapter 01 · What a Stock Really Is

## Hook

You bought a share of a company. Now you own a slice of a business. But the next
morning, the slice you bought for $100 is "worth" $96 — or $108. The company did
not change overnight. Its products, its customers, its factory, its debt, none of
it moved a millimeter. Yet your share now has a different price.

Most beginners find this confusing. They think a stock is a piece of paper, or an
icon on a phone app, whose price floats around for its own reasons. They buy and
sell based on the icon moving. They do not understand *why* the icon moves, so
they cannot tell a good move from a bad one — and that confusion costs them more
than anything else.

This chapter does one thing: it makes you understand what a stock **actually is**
in the eyes of the person who sells it to you, and why that one fact explains
almost everything you will learn in this book. Get this one idea, and the rest of
the book becomes a set of consequences rather than a list of rules.

## Concept

**Simply put: a stock is a tiny piece of ownership in a real, going business —
not a trading chip, and not a number that moves for its own sake.**

The single most important fact about a stock is this: the company itself does not
know, and does not care, that its shares trade at $96 or $108. A company's
operations are independent of the price its shares fetch on the market. Apple
makes the same number of phones whether its share is $200 or $260. This is the
central, slightly strange idea of the whole equity world, and it is the source of
almost every mistake beginners make.

There are two sides to every stock. Let us name them before we build on them.

**Ownership:**
- *Plain meaning:* When you buy a share, you become a tiny owner of the company,
  alongside the other owners. Your share is a certificate of that ownership,
  measured by the fraction of all shares that you hold.
- *Why it matters:* Because you own a fraction, you have a claim on part of the
  company's profits and, if it ever closes, part of what is left after debts are
  paid. That claim is the reason a stock has any value at all.
- *How to use it:* Always ask, "What did I actually buy — a piece of a business,
  or just a ticker?" The answer decides whether you are an owner or a speculator.

**The two prices:**
- *Plain meaning:* Every stock has two very different prices at the same time. The
  **market price** is what people will pay for your share right now on the
  market. The **value** is what the business is actually worth, the share of its
  future profits it will produce.
- *Why it matters:* These two prices are not the same number, and they rarely are.
  The market price swings on moods, news, and other people's moods. The value
  changes only when the business itself changes — when it earns more, or less.
  Confusing the two is the most common beginner error, and it is the theme of this
  whole book.
- *How to use it:* Before buying, separate the two questions. "How much is the
  company worth?" is a question about the business. "What will people pay today?"
  is a question about the crowd. Keep them apart.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** The price of a stock is set by the market. The value of a
stock is set by the business. The gap between the two — and your job as an
investor — is the entire subject of equity investing. Here is how the idea works,
step by step.

1. **A business produces cash.** Over its life, a good business generates profits
   and cash. This is the engine. Everything else in this book is about how that
   cash reaches you, and how much of it.
2. **Those future cash flows are what the stock is "really" worth.** A share's
   true value is the present value of all the cash it will hand back to its owners
   over the years — through dividends and through growth. (We will build the math
   in Book 10, Valuation. For now, hold the concept.)
3. **The market price is a vote by all the people trading right now.** It reflects
   today's news, today's moods, today's supply and demand. It can be above value,
   or below value, and it changes constantly for reasons that have nothing to do
   with the business's cash flows.
4. **The gap between price and value is where returns are made.** If you buy a
   business for less than it is worth, time tends to work for you: the price
   drifts up toward value. If you pay more than it is worth, time works against
   you, and you need a lucky or rising market to escape.
5. **The company is indifferent to its own share price.** It keeps making phones,
  treating patients, or serving customers, regardless of what its ticker says.
  This is why long-term owners and short-term traders are playing two different
  games — and why the long-term game is the only one an ordinary investor can win
  reliably.

## Historical cases

Let us anchor the idea in three moments that separate the "owner" view from the
"chip" view.

- **The 1929 crash, and the men who owned vs. men who traded.** In 1929 the Dow
  peaked near 381 and fell to about 41 by mid-1932 — a drop of roughly 89%. The
  men who were trading short-term chips with heavy margin lost everything; the
  men who had bought whole, sensible businesses at sensible prices and simply held
  through the panic were a different species of investor. The lesson is not that
  "stocks fall." The lesson is that the *price* can collapse while the *business*
  survives — and that the gap between the two is what the market was punishing.
- **Warren Buffett and the "be owner" doctrine, 1965 onward.** In his famous 1965
  letter, Buffett described the ideal owner as someone who treats the stock as a
  ticket to the cash flows of a business, not as a betting slip. Over decades this
  doctrine produced returns that consistently beat the market — not because Buffett
  predicted the icon, but because he bought whole businesses for less than they
  were worth and let the gap close. This is the "owner" view, made into a
  discipline.
- **The 2000 dot-com bust, the "chip" view on full display.** At its March 2000
  peak, the Nasdaq Composite was around 5,048; by October 2002 it had fallen to
  roughly 1,114 — a decline of about 78%. Many of those shares represented
  businesses that earned no profit at all; their market prices were pure chip-trading
  moods with no underlying cash flow to lean on. When the moods turned, the prices
  had nothing to hold them up. This is the "chip" view, and it is why those names
  lost 78%.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** The 1929 fall (381 → 41, about −89%) and the 2000
> dot-com fall (Nasdaq Composite 5,048 → about 1,114, about −78%) are
> index-to-index moves over years, not single-day events. They illustrate the
> gap between price and value, not a one-day crash. Treat them as long
> horizons.

## Numeric example

Suppose a company is worth $100 per share in the eyes of a careful owner, based
on the cash it will produce over the coming years. But the market today, in a
mildly fearful mood, is selling shares for $80. You buy at $80.

Two things can now happen. The market mood improves and the price drifts up toward
$100 — you have made $20 without the business changing a thing. Or the business
improves and its true value rises to $120 — now both the price and the value have
moved, and you have made even more. The key move is the first one: you made money
by buying at a *gap*, and you did not need the company to do anything new.

Now flip it. Suppose you buy a share the market prices at $130, but its true value
is only $100. You are now paying $30 more than the business is worth. For you to
end up whole, you need the business to get *better* by 30% — or the market to stay
foolish longer. Both are things you cannot control. This is why "buying below
value" is the one edge an ordinary investor can reliably hold, and "buying above
value" is the one trap that always eventually bites.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating the stock as the business.** When you say "I own Apple," you do not
   own the factory or the brand; you own a claim on a fraction of its future
   profits. The business runs regardless of your feelings. Treat it that way.
2. **Reading the price as the value.** A price of $100 is a vote, not a fact. If
   the business has not changed, a drop to $96 does not mean it got worse — it
   means the crowd got quieter. Confusing the two is the root of most selling at
   the wrong moment.
3. **Ignoring that two games exist.** The trader plays the short-term price game;
   the owner plays the long-term value game. You can mix them, but you must know
   which one you are in, or you will be punished by both without winning either.
4. **Forgetting that the company is indifferent.** The business does not know its
   ticker. Do not let the ticker's mood make decisions that a business owner would
   never make.

## Different contexts

- **For the everyday investor:** the "owner" view is the only one that is
  reproducible. You do not need to out-trade the market; you need to buy
  businesses for less than they are worth and let time close the gap. That is the
  entire strategy, in one sentence.
- **For the active trader:** the "chip" view is the game you are playing. Know its
  rules — momentum, liquidity, moods — but do not confuse winning at the chip game
  with owning anything. The two games reward different people, and the chip game
  is one where most retail participants lose over time.
- **For the beginner who just bought a stock:** do the one question that separates
  the two. "Am I buying a piece of a business, or a number that moves?" If you
  cannot answer the first question, you are in the chip game — and that is fine,
  as long as you know it and play it for small amounts you can afford to lose.

## Practical framework and summary

Here is the move list, in order, for every stock you ever consider:

1. **Name what you bought.** "A piece of a business," or "a trading chip." Write
   it down. This single naming decides everything that follows.
2. **Separate the two prices.** For the business, ask: what is it really worth?
   For the market, ask: what is the crowd paying today? Keep the two numbers in
   separate columns.
3. **Decide your game.** Owner or chip? Pick one deliberately. The owner's game
   is the only one that scales and only one that does not require outperforming
   everyone.
4. **Judge the gap.** If price is well below a reasonable estimate of value, you
   have an edge. If price is at or above value, you have no edge and must rely on
   the business improving or the crowd staying foolish — both out of your control.
5. **Check the business runs regardless.** Confirm that the company's operations
   are independent of its ticker. If your thesis depends on the ticker, you are in
   the chip game.

> **Decision rule — the one test for every stock.** Before you buy, write two
> numbers: the value you estimate, and the price the market is offering. If the
> price is meaningfully below your value, you are buying an edge; if it is at or
> above, you are buying a hope. Never buy a hope and call it an investment.

**Summary in three sentences:** A stock is a tiny piece of ownership in a real,
going business, not a trading chip. It has two prices at once — the market price,
set by the crowd, and the value, set by the business's cash — and the gap between
them is the whole game. Buy below value, let time close the gap, and stop
mistaking the crowd's mood for the business's worth.

**One thing you can do today:** Take one stock you already own, or one you are
thinking about buying, and write the two numbers — "what I think it is worth" and
"what the market offers." If the second is above the first, ask yourself whether
you are an owner or a speculator, and be honest about which one you are.

> 📌 **Sources and caveats.**
> - *Price vs. value.* The distinction between a stock's *market price* (set by supply, demand, and mood) and its *intrinsic value* (the present value of the business's future cash flows) is the foundational idea of equity investing, classically articulated by Benjamin Graham in *The Intelligent Investor* (1949). The chapter's "the market price is a *vote* by everyone trading right now" echoes Graham's famous line — "in the short run the market is a *voting machine*; in the long run it is a *weighing machine*."
> - *The price floats ($100 → $96 / $108) are illustrative*, not a specific stock; "Apple" is used as a *generic* device to show that a company's *operations* are independent of its *share price* — not a claim about any specific price.
