# Quantitative and Systematic Investing

# Chapter 01 · Why Systematic Investing Exists

## Hook

Most people meet *quantitative investing* through a chart of a backtest — a curve that rises, and rises, and rises, until it stops being a curve and becomes a promise. That picture is the most expensive one in the whole territory, because the rising curve is usually a story the past was always going to tell, not a forecast of what comes next. The single most important fact about this territory is that a great result on paper and a working strategy in the world are two different objects, and almost everyone who loses money has confused them.

This chapter does one thing: explain why a systematic approach exists and name the three traps that decide whether it survives, so that the next eleven chapters have somewhere to land. It states the central warning of the book and sharpens the no-prediction principle for this territory.

## Concept

**Simply put:** a systematic strategy is a *rule* that maps what the market or a signal shows you to a position you hold, and then acts on that rule without a human re-deciding each time. The rule replaces judgment at the point of action. A momentum strategy, for example, says "buy what has risen recently"; a value strategy says "buy what the market prices cheaply relative to some measure"; a rules-based rebalance says "move toward these weights, and no further." What makes it *systematic* is the rule, not the computer.

**the three objects the book separates:**

- *Signal:* a measurable quantity the strategy watches — a factor value, a price relationship, a statistical feature — that the rule turns into a position.
- *Backtest:* a replay of the rule over historical data, producing a performance curve from the past.
- *Reality:* what happens when the rule is run forward, on data that never existed when the rule was chosen.

**The point.** A signal, a backtest, and reality are not the same thing, and the gap between the last two is where strategies die. This book is the study of that gap.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** three forces define every systematic strategy, and they are the spine of the book:

1. **The backtest is not reality.** A backtest is a story told backward, on data the rule already saw. It is evidence, not a guarantee.
2. **A signal is not a proven mechanism.** Finding a number that correlates with returns is not explaining why. A signal without a mechanism is a guess with extra steps.
3. **Significance is not tradable.** A statistically significant edge can be too small, too unstable, or too costly to earn after fees and friction. Proof and profit are different tests.

Hold these three and you can read almost any strategy claim correctly; drop one and you are back to the rising-curve picture.

## Historical cases

- **The 1960s roots of factor research.** The idea that measurable characteristics of firms (small size, value, momentum) relate to returns was formalized through the 1960s and 1970s. The lesson: signals existed long before computers could trade them, and a signal is a hypothesis about the market, not a trade. `illustrative`
- **1987 and the failure of a "normal" world.** When markets moved far outside the recent range, many systematic and model-based approaches that had looked stable in backtests performed very poorly, because the data they trusted had never seen such a move. The lesson: a backtest is only as old as the calm it covered. `illustrative`
- **The 2008 crisis as a regime shock.** Diversification and model-based risk measures that looked solid in prior years strained or failed as correlations jumped toward one. The lesson: regimes change, and a strategy validated in one regime is unvalidated in the next. `illustrative`

## Numeric example

Suppose a strategy returns 12% per year on a backtest over a 15-year calm period. That number is a property of those 15 years, not of the future. If the strategy also depends on a market regime that has now changed, the 12% is not a forecast at all — it is the score of a game the market is no longer playing. The same curve, read as "what the past did" instead of "what the future will," is the only honest way to hold it. `(illustrative estimate; figures vary by source)`

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the backtest as a forecast.** The curve is a backward story. It is evidence about the rule, not a promise about tomorrow.
- **Confusing a signal with an explanation.** A correlation is not a mechanism. A signal that has no economic reason to persist is one that can stop.
- **Treating significance as profit.** A result can be statistically real and still untradeable after costs, slippage, and capacity.
- **Fitting the data instead of the idea.** A strategy tuned until the curve is perfect has usually memorized the past and will fail the next test.
- **Ignoring regime change.** A strategy that worked in calm water can fail in a storm, and backtests are usually built in calm water.

## Different contexts

- **For the individual investor,** a systematic approach is a discipline — a way to act on a rule without re-deciding at every emotional moment. Its value is consistency, not magic.
- **For the portfolio manager,** a systematic approach is an infrastructure — data, signals, risk measures, and a process that can be tested and audited.
- **For the skeptic,** a systematic approach is a target — every rising curve claims an edge, and the job of the reader is to ask which of the three forces it survived.
- **For the builder of models,** a systematic approach is a living system — something that must be re-validated as regimes and data quality change.

The same "12% backtest" is none of these to itself; it is whichever one you choose to read it as.

## Practical framework and summary

The moves:

1. **Separate the signal from the mechanism.** Before trusting a number, ask why it might persist. A correlation without a reason is not an edge.
2. **Treat the backtest as evidence, not a forecast.** Ask what the curve is a property of, and whether that is still true.
3. **Demand significance and tradability separately.** A real result is not necessarily a profitable one after costs.
4. **Test for regime change.** Ask what the strategy does when the world that produced it stops being the world.

**Decision rule:** if a strategy's case rests only on a rising backtest curve, you have a story, not a strategy — and a story is not evidence that it will continue.

**If–then:** if a strategy that looked strong in the backtest behaves badly out-of-sample, then the edge was probably in the data or the regime, not in the idea.

**Summary in three sentences:** A systematic strategy is a rule that turns signals into positions, and its whole difficulty is the gap between a backtest and reality. The three forces — backtest is not reality, signal is not mechanism, significance is not tradable — are the spine of the book and the test of every claim. The discipline is to read every rising curve as a backward story and to ask which forces it survived.

**One thing you can do today:** for the last "great backtest" you saw, write down which of the three forces it had not yet proven it could survive.
