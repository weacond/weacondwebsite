# Asset Allocation and Portfolio Construction

# Chapter 01 · From a Single Investment to a Portfolio

## Hook

Every chapter so far has looked at one thing at a time: one stock, one bond, one fund, one property. That is how you learn what each asset does. But no one lives inside one asset. You hold a portfolio, and a portfolio is not the sum of its parts, because the parts move together and the togetherness changes everything.

This chapter does one thing: explain why the portfolio, not the individual holding, is the real unit of investment.

## Concept

A portfolio is a set of holdings whose risk and return depend on how the holdings move relative to one another. The single most important property is *correlation*: when two holdings fall together, diversification gives nothing; when they do not, it gives a great deal.

**Simply put:** a portfolio is a correlation machine; its return is the weighted average of its parts, but its risk is not, because risk depends on how the parts move together.

**portfolio:**
- *Plain meaning:* a collection of investments held together, judged on its own risk and return.
- *Why it matters:* the portfolio, not the single holding, is what you actually live with, and it can be safer or riskier than any one part.
- *How to use it:* judge a holding by how it behaves inside the portfolio, not on its own.

**The point.** You do not invest in assets; you invest in a portfolio, and its properties are emergent, not the sum of the parts.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** two properties combine, and they behave differently.

1. **Return is additive.** The portfolio's return is the weighted average of its parts' returns.
2. **Risk is not additive.** The portfolio's risk depends on correlation; low correlation lowers portfolio risk below the weighted average of the parts' risks.
3. **Diversification** is the reduction of risk that comes from holding things that do not move together.

## Historical cases

- **A 60/40 portfolio versus all-in stocks (worked).** A portfolio 60% in stocks (volatility 15%) and 40% in bonds (volatility 8%) with low correlation can have volatility near 10%, below the 15% of an all-stock portfolio. The lesson: mixing lowers risk more than the weights suggest.
- **A portfolio of two stocks that fall together (worked).** Two stocks each with 15% volatility but moving together (correlation 1) produce a portfolio with 15% volatility; there is no diversification. The lesson: correlation, not the number of holdings, creates the benefit.
- **The 2008 crisis, when correlations rose (illustrative).** In a system-wide crisis, assets that normally fall together fell together, and diversification failed exactly when it was most needed. The lesson: the benefit is real, but it can evaporate in a crisis.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** the portfolio volatilities are worked illustrations; the 2008 episode is illustrative.

## Numeric example

Show the difference between additive return and non-additive risk.

- **Return:** 60% of a 10% asset and 40% of a 5% asset gives 0.6 × 10% + 0.4 × 5% = 8% — a weighted average.
- **Risk, no correlation:** the portfolio volatility is about 9.6%, well below the 15% of an all-stock position.
- **Risk, perfect correlation:** the portfolio volatility is about 12.2%, the weighted average — the benefit vanishes.

**Decision rule:** the return you can predict from the weights; the risk you must get from the correlations.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding the risks of the parts.** Risk does not add; it depends on correlation.
- **Counting holdings as diversification.** Fifty correlated holdings diversify nothing.
- **Judging a holding in isolation.** A holding that is risky on its own can lower a portfolio's risk.
- **Forgetting that correlations rise in a crisis.** The diversification benefit is not available when you most want it.

## Different contexts

- **For a long-term portfolio** low correlation across asset classes is the main source of the benefit, and it is worth paying for.
- **For a short-term portfolio** the benefit matters less, because the holding period is too short for correlations to matter much.
- **In a crisis** correlations rise and the benefit shrinks, so the portfolio that looks safe in calm markets can fail in a crisis.

## Practical framework and summary

The moves:
1. Stop judging a single holding on its own; judge it inside the portfolio.
2. Recognize that return is a weighted average but risk is not.
3. Look for low correlation between the parts, because that is what creates the benefit.
4. Remember that correlations rise in a crisis, and size the portfolio for that.

**Decision rule:** the portfolio is the unit of investment, and its risk is set by how the parts move together.

**If–then:** if two parts move together, they add no diversification; if they do not, they lower the portfolio's risk below the weighted average of their risks.

**Summary in three sentences:** A portfolio is not the sum of its parts: its return is the weighted average of the parts' returns, but its risk depends on how the parts move together. Correlation is the source of the diversification benefit, and it can rise in a crisis when you need it most. The single shift this book asks of you is to judge every holding inside the portfolio, not on its own.

**One thing you can do today:** look at your two largest holdings and ask whether they tend to move together or apart; if they move together, you hold less diversification than you think.
