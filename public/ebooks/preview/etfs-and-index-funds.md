# ETFs and Index Funds

# Chapter 01 · What Is an Index: A Recipe for the Market

## Hook

You have already heard the sentence "the stock market went up." But there is no single thing called "the stock market." There are thousands of exchanges, and millions of shares moving at once. When the news says "the market rose 1%," it is really pointing to one specific list — a defined collection of stocks, a recipe — and reporting how that recipe did today. That list is an **index**.

The most famous recipe is the one the world quotes as "the market": the large-capital US stock index, the S&P 500. It holds 500 companies, but the top ten can be close to a third of it, and the very largest single name can be worth more than the next two or three hundred combined. So "owning the market" is not the same as "owning 500 companies equally." It is owning one recipe, and the recipe has weights, rules, and a date when it is rewritten.

This chapter does one thing: teach you what an index actually is — not the label, but the construction — so that later, when we buy a fund that "tracks an index," you will know exactly what you own and what rules quietly move inside it.

## Concept

**Simply put:** an index is a fixed set of securities combined with a fixed rule for weighting them. It is a recipe, not a basket of food. The recipe is public, mechanical, and dated — it tells you which ingredients, in what proportions, and it is published.

**Index:**

- *Plain meaning:* a published, rules-based list of securities combined with weighting rules, used as a benchmark and as a thing to own.
- *Why it matters:* you cannot "buy the market" without first choosing which recipe you mean, and every recipe has rules that quietly shift what you own.
- *How to use it:* before you trust "I own the market," ask which index, what its weighting rule is, and when it is rebalanced.

**Construction:**

- *Plain meaning:* the three choices that build an index: which securities are in it (selection), how they are weighted (weighting), and when the list is rewritten (reconstitution).
- *Why it matters:* two indices can cover "the same" market and behave very differently because their construction differs.
- *How to use it:* compare any two "market" indices by their construction, not by their name.

**The point.** An index is a mechanical recipe. The three levers — selection, weighting, reconstitution — are the only three places where a "simple" index can behave like a complicated one, and the next four chapters live on each of these levers.

## Principle

**The mechanism:** an index is defined by three independent choices, and each choice has a hidden cost or surprise.

1. **Selection** decides who is in. A "total market" index is still a selection — it includes most, not all, of a market, and it must define what "most" means (size, liquidity, domicile). A sector or "growth" index is a selection too. Selection is where style lives.
2. **Weighting** decides how much of each. The most common rule is **market-cap weighting**: weight by size, so the biggest companies dominate. Alternatives include **equal weighting** (everyone gets the same share) and **factor/smart-beta weighting** (weight by a characteristic like value or low-volatility). Weighting is where concentration and style live.
3. **Reconstitution** decides when the recipe is rewritten. Indices are rebalanced and reselected on a schedule (often quarterly or twice a year). Reconstitution is where the fund is forced to trade, and where "the recipe changed under me" happens.

The hidden surprise is that each choice is mechanical but not free: selection can drift toward a style, weighting can concentrate you in a few names, and reconstitution forces trades at scheduled times.

## Historical cases

**Case 1 — The market-cap tilt of a "diversified" index.** The S&P 500 is cap-weighted, so a handful of the largest companies can make up a large share of the index; in recent years the top ten have approached a third of the total. The lesson: "owning 500 companies" can feel like owning a handful, because the recipe hands most of the weight to the biggest names. The magnitude is index-specific and year-specific; the principle — cap-weighting concentrates in the largest names — is the durable fact. Source: standard index construction; the exact top-ten share is `illustrative`.

**Case 2 — A reconstitution day that forces selling.** When a company falls out of an index, the index-linked funds must sell it; the pressure of many funds selling on the same scheduled date can push the price down further on that day, a small self-fulfilling move. The lesson: mechanical rules create mechanical trading flows, and those flows can nudge prices on rebalance days. Source: academic literature on index rebalancing effects; exact magnitude `illustrative`.

**Case 3 — Equal-weight versus cap-weight, when the big names fall.** If the largest name in a cap-weighted index loses 30% in a year while the rest rise 10%, the cap-weighted index is dragged down hard because that name carries so much weight; an equal-weight index (every name 1/500) is barely touched. This is why "owning the market" and "owning the market equally" can diverge sharply in a year when the biggest name breaks. Source: construction logic; magnitudes illustrative.

> 📌 **Note on the numbers:** the exact top-ten share of a large-cap index, and the exact price effect of a reconstitution day, vary by index and year and are marked `illustrative` or illustrative. The principles — cap-weighting concentrates, reconstitution forces scheduled trades, equal-weighting is less sensitive to any one name — hold regardless of the exact figures.

## Numeric example

The same 10% return on every name gives the same index return no matter the weighting — that is the easy case. The interesting case is when the names differ. Take five names in a 50/25/15/7/3 cap structure, and an equal-weight 20/20/20/20/20 alternative:

- If every name returns +10%, both portfolios return +10%. Weighting does not matter when returns are equal.
- Now the top name (50% of the cap-weighted index) falls −30%, the other four rise +10%.
  - Cap-weighted: 0.50 × (−0.30) + 0.50 × 0.10 = −15% + 5% = **−10.0%**.
  - Equal-weighted: 0.20 × (−0.30) + 0.80 × 0.10 = −6% + 8% = **+2.0%**.

Same five names, same year, one portfolio down 10%, the other up 2%. The difference is not the market — it is the weighting rule. That is the whole reason "how the index is built" matters: it decides how much one name's bad day can hurt you.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistake 1 — "Own the market" as if it were one thing.** There is no single market; you are choosing a recipe. A total-market index, a large-cap index, and a sector index are all "markets" that behave differently.
- **Mistake 2 — Assuming "500 companies" means 500 equal slices.** Cap-weighting means the biggest names dominate; your "500 companies" can be 10 companies with a long tail of near-zero weight.
- **Mistake 3 — Forgetting the recipe rewrites itself.** Reconstitution is scheduled and mechanical; your holdings change on dates you did not choose, and that change is not "you did something."
- **Mistake 4 — Reading an index as static.** Selection drifts; a company can fall out for size or liquidity reasons, and a new one can be added, changing the style without any announcement you read.

## Different contexts

- **Investing vs trading the index itself:** owning an index fund for decades vs trying to trade the index up and down. The recipe is built for the former; the trading version pays costs on every rebalance you ride.
- **US vs global vs sector indices:** the weighting rule is the same idea, but the selection differs — a US index is US companies, a global index adds other countries, a sector index narrows to one industry. The construction principle is identical; the contents are not.
- **Cap-weight vs equal-weight vs smart-beta:** three weighting rules, three different risk profiles. Equal-weighting is more sensitive to small names; smart-beta tilts toward a characteristic. Each is a legitimate recipe with a different personality — covered in Chapters 04 and 11.
- **Index as benchmark vs index as product:** you use the index to judge whether a manager beat "the market," and you can also buy the index itself. The same recipe serves two masters — judge, and thing-to-own — and that dual role is the source of most confusion.

## Practical framework and summary

The moves:

1. **Name the recipe.** When you hear "the market," ask which index — total, large-cap, sector, global, factor.
2. **Read the weighting.** Cap-weighted concentrates in the largest names; equal-weighted spreads; smart-beta tilts. The weighting decides how much one name can hurt you.
3. **Note the reconstitution.** The list is rewritten on a schedule; that forces trades and can nudge prices on those dates.
4. **Separate the judge from the product.** An index can measure a manager's performance and be a thing you own; do not confuse "the benchmark I am measured against" with "the thing I bought."

**Decision rule:** before you say "I own the market," state which index, what its weighting rule is, and when it is reconstituted. If you cannot, you do not know what you own.

**If–then:** If two "market" indices behave differently, it is because their construction (selection, weighting, or reconstitution) differs — not because one is "more the market."

**Summary in three sentences:**
- An index is a mechanical recipe: a selection of securities, a weighting rule, and a reconstitution schedule.
- The three levers are where a "simple" index hides its complexity — selection carries style, weighting carries concentration, reconstitution forces scheduled trades.
- "Owning the market" is really owning one recipe, and knowing its rules is knowing what you actually hold.

**One thing you can do today:** pick the index you have heard most and find its fact sheet — not its recent return, but its weighting method and its rebalance date. That single fact tells you more about what "owning it" means than a decade of performance charts.
